Life's a Beach

And other observations from a city that takes the sea seriously

Sydney, New South Wales, Australia · 29 November – 6 December 2016

“No wonder they look so damned happy.”

Bill Bryson · Down Under

We stayed near Bronte Beach, and what I remember from the first morning is the noise of rainbow lorikeets in a Norfolk Island hibiscus at the top of Pacific Street, on the road down to the sea.

Sydney is not really about beauty in the European sense. It is about the sea. The beaches are held in coves between sandstone headlands — not the long open beaches of Southern California, but contained, each with its own character. We walked the six kilometres from Coogee to Bondi along the coast, passing snorkellers and divers at Gordon's Bay. The walk involved more stone staircases than I had expected; Hannes was in the pram, and at several points I had to carry both him and the pram down the steps. Mackenzies Bay, between Bondi and Tamarama, is said to become a beach once every seven years and was due that summer, though we saw only the rocks. Bondi itself was too crowded for us, but Tamarama, halfway along, was the right size.

Sydney lives by the sea even when the sea is out of sight. At a crossing in Bondi Junction, a good half-hour's walk from the water, people were waiting for the lights in board shorts and flip-flops, as if the beach were just around the corner — which, in their minds, it probably was.

We did the harbour, of course. Waverley, where we stayed, was older than we had expected — arched windows, plastered façades. But the Sydney we remember was the coast.

Then it turned out our flat had cockroaches. Our host did not seem unduly bothered and pointed cheerfully to a can of insect spray under the sink. I killed three over the course of the week, and we kept the bedroom door shut at night, and it remained unpleasant.

Practical notes

The shallow end of Bronte Beach is partially enclosed by rocks, forming a natural pool that's gentler for small children than the open surf. An older man we passed on the path down told us, the first morning, that he never swims before Christmas — the water is still cold so early in the season.
