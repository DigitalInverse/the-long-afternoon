An Olive Tree, A Grapevine, A Boat

What remains of Greece, when you take the rest away

Divarata, Kefalonia · 27 June – 4 July 2025

“If you deconstruct Greece, in the end you will see an olive tree, a grapevine, and a boat remain. With this much, you can reconstruct her.”

Odysseas Elytis

We had spent earlier summers in Italy — in Liguria, Sicily, Sardinia, Elba. Italy is wonderful, but something usually goes wrong: a parking ticket, the rental car, a closed museum, a restaurant that takes lunch off when we are hungriest. On Kefalonia, nothing went wrong. The car worked. You could park almost anywhere along the road. The restaurants stayed open through the afternoon. After the first few days I stopped waiting for the small disaster.

We rented a house above the bay where Myrtos lies, on a slope a few minutes’ drive from a village called Divarata — small enough that its four good restaurants felt extravagant, and over the week we worked our way through the classics: gemista, moussaka, souvlaki. The balcony looked west. The sunsets were the kind people fly to Santorini for, except there were no other people — only the sound of sheep bells in the hills below, faint and unhurried, the way you imagine sheep bells must once have sounded everywhere. The skies caught fire. I had lived in northern New Mexico for five winters and thought I knew what evening light could do; Kefalonia did the same trick, only over water.

Days followed a pattern. We drove down to Lithovatis Beach, a small bay with a handful of locals and the occasional other family, and snorkelled in clear water until lunch. Then we walked the few minutes up to Sea Rock and ate outside — souvlaki for Hannes, and a salad I keep thinking about. After lunch we went back down.

Fiskardo, on the northern tip, is the village the guidebooks photograph: pastel houses with shutters in pale greens and blues, a harbour full of small boats, bougainvillea hanging over café awnings. The Venetian centuries left their fingerprints on the architecture, and we noticed, sitting at a café, that most of the tourists around us seemed to be Italian. They had crossed a sea to find a quieter version of their own coast. We had ice cream there once and watched the boats. But the place we kept returning to was Lithovatis, in Agia Effimia — quieter, less photographed, the essence of the island as we found it.

What stays with me, though, is not any one beach. It is the strangers. Every waiter, every shopkeeper, every person who handed us a key or pointed us down a road did so with a kind of warmth that has become rare in places that see many tourists. They were not performing service; they were just being people, and we happened to be the people in front of them. We left the island lighter than we arrived.

Practical notes

The hike down to Fteri Beach takes about an hour from the small parking area above. It is steep but well-trodden, ending in a short scramble down a dry riverbed; most other visitors arrive by water taxi. Sea Rock, on the road above Lithovatis Beach in Agia Effimia, serves a salad of rocket and other leafy greens with grilled peaches and grilled talagani.
