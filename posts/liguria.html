La Dolce Vita

What ten days in Liguria taught me about the Italian good life

Lerici and Sestri Levante, Liguria · 5–15 July 2024

“Our actual happiness with, and in, a place must be a brief and apparently haphazard phenomenon: an interval in which we achieve receptivity to the world around us.”

Alain de Botton · The Art of Travel

We were parked illegally on a narrow cliffside road between Fiascherino and Tellaro, trying to decide whether the Italian good life was worth a parking ticket. The sea below us was impossibly blue, folding into the rocks in slow, patient waves. The air smelled of salt and sun-warmed stone. It was the kind of view that appears on postcards and persuades people to move countries.

And yet, inside our car, my wife and I were anxious.

“Can we park here?”

“There’s no sign.”

“Maybe that’s worse.”

It was our third day of ten, and already Italy was defeating our itinerary. I had thought I understood this country. I thought I understood the idea of La Dolce Vita — the sweet life. Leisure. Pleasure. Sunlight. Espresso. A kind of elegant indifference to urgency.

But in that moment, I was not living sweetly. I was calculating fines.

We had imagined Fiascherino as a simple idyll: swim, sun, perhaps a late lunch overlooking the water. We had not imagined parking as the central drama of the day. As we hesitated, two Italian women pulled up and parked in front of us — also at the side of the road. We stepped out and asked, in hesitant Italian, whether they knew the rules. They laughed. They were tourists too. They had no idea. For a moment the four of us stood there, united by uncertainty. We examined the absence of signs as though it might reveal legal wisdom.

Then one of them shrugged, smiled, and said, “Well, let’s go to the beach and have fun.”

No further analysis. No collective risk assessment. Just a decision to let the day happen. And suddenly I understood something I hadn’t grasped on my previous trips to Italy. La Dolce Vita is not about aesthetic pleasure alone. It is not the cobblestones or the espresso cups or the curated beauty of coastal villages. It is a posture toward uncertainty. A willingness to trade minor control for major experience. The car might get a ticket. The day might contain inconvenience. The world might not cooperate with our itinerary.

But the sea was waiting.

We went to the beach. The water was warm and clear and indifferent to our deliberations. We swam, we dried ourselves on sun-baked rocks, we forgot about the car. When we returned hours later, there was no ticket. But that almost felt beside the point.

Liguria taught me that the good life is not the absence of small worries. It is the choice, sometimes, to set them down.

Practical notes

The hike to the abbey at San Fruttuoso takes about two hours along the coast; the water in the cove at the end is exceptionally clear, and worth the walk. Not eating pizza at Kicks in Santa Margherita Ligure is a small tragedy. For quicker meals, the focaccia or porchetta sandwiches at Casalini Punto Caldo in central Lerici are excellent. Parking in small coastal villages like Bogliasco and the Cinque Terre is nearly impossible; the train and bus are easier than a rental car.
