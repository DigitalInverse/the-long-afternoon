Slightly in Love

Our first journey together, in the most beautiful city we had ever seen

Paris, France · 29 August – 1 September 2014

“Why don't you start living your life in Paris?”

Ernest Hemingway · The Sun Also Rises

Late August in Paris empties the city. The Parisians leave for the coast, and what stays behind is the architecture, the light, and a thinner crowd of foreigners. It was our first journey together, and neither of us had been here before. We had a hotel just off the Champs-Élysées and four days to use.

The city is beautiful in a way we had not been prepared for. Other cities have their charms — narrower, older, more lived-in — but no other city we had seen was so deliberately composed. Haussmann's beige palaces along every boulevard, the slate mansard roofs, the wrought-iron balconies repeating from corner to corner. The plane trees lining the avenues in even rows. From the top of the Arc de Triomphe the streets radiate out in a clean star, and the eye follows them all the way to La Défense. From the Eiffel Tower the city lies flat and pale and goes on forever.

So we walked. Forty kilometres in four days, which is fortunate, since both of us like walking. We crossed the Seine and recrossed it. We sat in the gardens at Place des Vosges, where the brick façades are the oldest of any square in the city. We climbed the Eiffel Tower and Hanna would not stand on the glass floor outside the second-level restaurant. We stood on the terrace at the Trocadéro at the right time of day, when the light comes from behind and the tower turns to silhouette. At dusk we walked down the Champs-Élysées toward the Arc, with the traffic thickening and the trees in two long colonnades, and the city held the last light a long time.

The small things stayed too. The bouillabaisse that arrived with the scales still on the fish and the tentacles still attached, which we had not been warned about. The jewellery shop near Place Vendôme where two locked doors stood between the street and the diamonds. The rabbits living wild outside the Musée de l'Armée, like something from Alice in Wonderland, and the policeman who blew his whistle the moment we stepped onto the grass to photograph them. The maître d' at Maison Blanche, who let us in to photograph the tower from his terrace after the restaurant had closed for the evening. We had been told the Parisians would be rude. They were not.

On the Pont des Arts we left a padlock among the thousands of others. I had ordered it before we left home — a heart-shaped lock, our names engraved on the face of it — and Hanna did not know about it until we were standing on the bridge. The railings sagged with the weight of all the locks before ours. We took a photograph of it. We were supposed to throw the key into the river. We kept it.

We came home tired and pleased and slightly in love with the city, which is, I suppose, what Paris is for.

Practical notes

We stayed at La Maison Champs-Élysées, a small five-star hotel just off the avenue. Walking is the way to see Paris. A round from Les Invalides to Sacré-Cœur — via the Eiffel Tower, the Trocadéro, the Arc de Triomphe, the Louvre, the Pont des Arts, Notre-Dame, and the Place des Vosges — takes around three hours and forty-five minutes on foot, best spread over several days, with proper stops at each place. Comfortable shoes, as in any large city.
