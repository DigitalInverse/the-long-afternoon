Saudade

Longing for a place while you are still in it

Lisbon, Portugal · 6–16 June 2016

“Where the land ends and the sea begins.”

Luís de Camões · Os Lusíadas

Lisbon in June was bright. The light bounced off the pale walls and the polished cobbles, and the city seemed to hum with it. The hills were steep enough that the trams climbed them, and a long red bridge crossed the Tagus toward the south. I kept thinking of San Francisco — a city built on the same kind of geography, the same bracing sense of being perched at the edge of a continent.

We rented a loft flat in Chiado with a small terrace, up fifty-five steps of a staircase so narrow that the pram had to be lifted, folded. Hannes was six months old. It was his first time abroad.

We stopped at the miradouros and looked out over the rooftops. At Mercado da Ribeira we ate a goat's cheese salad with a scoop of honey ice cream, raspberries, blackberries, and mixed nuts — Hanna still remembers it as the best of her life. We took a bus out to Praia do Guincho one day — a beach for brave surfers in wetsuits, not really for small children — where the wind off the Atlantic was strong and the water cold.

From Cais do Sodré the train follows the river out to Belém. We ate pastéis de nata at the bakery near the monastery, the custard still warm. The Padrão dos Descobrimentos rises from the riverbank — Henry the Navigator at the prow, the explorers behind him in stone — pointed at an empire that has been gone for a long time.

There is a Portuguese word, saudade, that is usually translated as longing — but it is more specific than that. It is the ache of missing something while knowing you cannot have it back. Saudade in its full sense belongs to the Portuguese — to fado, to the long sailings, to centuries of return. What I felt in Lisbon was smaller. We would come back, of course. But the next time we came back, Hannes would walk. The pram would not need to be lifted, folded, up a narrow staircase. The trip we were on would have already happened.

I remember the wheels of the pram clattering across the polished stones, and the old trams rattling past every few minutes, and worrying mostly about whether it was all too much for Hannes, facing forward.

Practical notes

Lisbon has a lot of stairs, especially up to the miradouros, and a carrier is more practical than a pram if you bring small children. Mercado da Ribeira is now also known as Time Out Market. Pastel de bacalhau and sardines in oil are worth seeking out; Hannes ate the sardines with surprising appetite.
