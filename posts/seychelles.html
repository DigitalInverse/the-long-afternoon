The Trip We Still Talk About

[Subtitle to be added]

Praslin, Seychelles · 8–18 February 2015

We first saw Anse Lazio as a turquoise shimmer through the aeroplane window, passing over Praslin on the descent into Mahé. We had read about it in a magazine. The sand, it said, felt like vanilla mousse between your toes. We had eighteen hours of flying behind us via Abu Dhabi and a missed connection ahead of us, and from the air the beach looked like something that could not possibly be real.

By the time we got there it was already evening. Our luggage had not arrived. We swam in our underwear in water the temperature of a warm bath. The guesthouse owner was in Australia; her niece and a friend looked after us. While we waited for our bags to be found and forwarded, Anders was given an ugly t-shirt and Hanna was given a beautiful sarong. The fruit bats came out at dusk.

The guesthouse stood in a garden of its own, set back from the beach. In the mornings we ate on a terrace that looked out at the Indian Ocean, with a few land crabs at the edge of the grass. We climbed up to the highest point on Praslin one day, near three hundred metres, through the kind of humidity that soaks a shirt before you have begun. A Rasta living halfway up the hill laughed at the state of my t-shirt. On the way down we got a lift from a couple of Indian tourists. Most days we did not leave the beach. There was shade under the palms, snorkelling among the granite boulders, and somewhere to eat without having to leave the sand. A curious surgeonfish once nipped Hanna on the thigh.

We took the boat to La Digue on Hanna's birthday and rented bicycles. Grand Anse on the east coast — turquoise water, white sand, granite boulders worn round by the sea — was visually as perfect as a beach could be, though the currents made swimming dangerous. Petit Anse, just over the headland, was smaller and almost empty. On the west coast was Anse Source d'Argent, famous from photographs and a little disappointing in person — too shallow to swim in, with seagrass on the bottom — but it was where we hid the key to a love-lock we had left on the Pont des Arts in Paris six months earlier. Three months after we left Seychelles, the city of Paris cut all the locks off the bridge.

We have talked about that trip a lot over the years. Partly it is the place — there are not many beaches in the world where the magazine writers turn out to have been telling the truth. Partly it is the timing. Hannes was born ten months after we came home.

It rained on the last night. We woke to the sound of it on the roof, and were glad, because we were leaving in the morning anyway. We had a last swim in the ocean before the boat to the airport, and the Madagascar weavers came to the breakfast terrace for the last time, as they had every morning, looking for crumbs.
